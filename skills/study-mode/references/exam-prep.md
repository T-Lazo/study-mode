# Exam and assessment preparation

Different goal from learning. Learning builds durable understanding; exam prep allocates finite time against a weighted assessment. Both matter, and confusing them wastes the run-up.

## Working backwards

1. **Fix the dates.** Every assessment for the block, in one list, with its weight from `BLOCK.md`.
2. **Find the shape.** Past papers, the module handbook, the marking scheme. What is actually assessed, and how? A module taught as theory and assessed by implementation needs different preparation from its lectures.
3. **Find the recurring topics.** Past papers repeat themselves more than anyone admits. Three papers is usually enough to see the pattern.
4. **Rank.** Weight × how shaky × how likely to appear. High weight and shaky is where the time goes. Solid and low weight gets nothing, however satisfying it is to revise.
5. **Schedule.** Real hours, in the calendar, with commitments and rest already in it. A timetable that assumes clear days is a timetable that gets abandoned in week one.

## Past papers, properly

The highest-value activity available and the most commonly done wrong.

**Wrong:** read the question, read the answer, feel prepared.
**Right:** timed, closed-book, written out fully, then marked against the scheme.

Do at least one under full exam conditions — same duration, no notes, no interruptions. The failure mode it surfaces is almost never knowledge. It is time allocation: 40 minutes on the first question and 10 on the last.

Calculate minutes-per-mark before starting and hold to it. When time is up on a question, stop, even mid-sentence.

## Condensed revision notes

Build one condensed set per subject, from memory first, then corrected against the source.

The direction matters. Making notes *from* the material is transcription — low value, feels productive. Making them from memory and then correcting them is retrieval practice with a built-in gap check. Same artifact, completely different activity.

Formats that work: spider diagrams for relationships, one-page summaries per topic, worked examples of the three question types that keep appearing. Flashcards only for genuinely atomic facts — definitions, complexities, syntax. Never for anything with structure.

## In the exam

- Read the whole paper first, both sides. Note question count and marks.
- Underline what each question actually asks. "Compare" is not "describe", and answering the wrong verb loses marks that the knowledge would have earned.
- Outline before writing on anything long-form.
- Keep rough work labelled and separate.

## Cramming

Sometimes the assessment is tomorrow and the run-up is gone. Triage beats principle at that point.

Say once, briefly, that this is the expensive way and it will not survive the week. Then help properly: rank by weight × likelihood, drill the top three topics with active recall, work one past paper timed. Do not spend the remaining hours on a lecture about spacing — that conversation belongs at the start of the next block, and the log will make the case better than you can.

Afterwards, log honestly. A topic crammed and passed is `conf 1`, not `conf 3`. It will need rebuilding, and the log is the only thing that will remember that.
