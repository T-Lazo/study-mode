# Reading academic material

Most wasted study time is reading. It feels productive, produces fluency, and often serves no stated goal. These protocols scope a reading before it starts.

## Three questions, before opening anything

Answer all three out loud. This costs thirty seconds and routinely saves an hour.

1. **Why am I reading this?** To answer a specific question, to prepare for a lecture, to find something for an assignment, to fill a gap the log flagged. "Because it was assigned" is not a reason — push for the real one.
2. **How should I read this?** Skim for structure, read one section closely, or work through it line by line. These are different activities with different costs.
3. **How long am I giving it?** A number, before starting. Not an estimate of how long it will take — a limit on how long it gets.

Question 3 is the one that matters most here. Reading expands to fill available time, and a reading with no time box is the most reliable way for a session to lose its outcome.

## SQ3R

For dense material that has to be retained rather than referenced.

**Survey** — headings, figures, first and last paragraphs, summary. Two minutes. Build the skeleton before the detail; without it, everything lands as unconnected facts.

**Question** — turn each heading into a question. "Type inference" becomes "how does the compiler infer a type without an annotation?". Now the reading has targets.

**Read** — read to answer those questions. Skip what does not serve them. Permission to skip is the point.

**Recite** — close the material and answer the questions aloud, in your own words. This is the retrieval step and the only one that builds memory. It is also the one everyone drops.

**Review** — self-quiz later, from the log, not from the text.

If a step is going to get dropped, drop Survey. Never drop Recite.

## Annotation that earns its place

Marginal notes only, and only these four kinds:

- A question the text raised and did not answer
- A connection to something already known
- A contradiction with another source or with the lecture
- A one-line restatement in your own words

That is it. If an annotation is not one of the four, it is highlighting with extra steps.

**Do not highlight.** It produces recognition, and recognition is not knowledge. If the reader is highlighting to mark what to come back to, a two-word margin note does the same job and forces a decision about what it is.

## Reading around lectures

- **Dense or unfamiliar material** — read after the lecture. The lecture supplies the mental model; reading first means processing detail with nothing to attach it to.
- **Familiar material, or a lecture that assumes preparation** — read before, at survey depth only. You want the skeleton, not the content.

Either way, one pass. A second full pass is rereading, which is rated low-utility. If something did not land, retrieve it and find the specific gap instead of re-running the whole text.

## Scope discipline

Reading is where tangents breed. Every interesting reference, footnote, and adjacent concept is an invitation away from the stated outcome.

Park them all. A reading session that ends with six parked items and one outcome met is a good session. One that ends with nine tabs open and the outcome untouched is the failure this mode exists to prevent.
